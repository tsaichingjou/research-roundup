Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices

King, Thomas B.; Feldman, Corey

This paper quantifies how uncertainty regarding the central bank's reaction function—termed monetary-policy risk (MPR)—impacts equilibrium asset pricing. Analyzing US financial data from 1994 to 2026, the authors show that asset price volatility is significantly higher outside FOMC blackout periods, with MPR contributing 1.7 percentage points to quarterly equity volatility and 12 basis points to 10-year Treasury yield volatility. A calibrated non-linear New Keynesian model demonstrates that MPR contributes roughly 40 basis points to the average 10-year nominal term premium and 120 basis points to the equity premium, with asset-pricing consequences further magnified at the effective lower bound.

Key takeaways

■ Monetary-policy risk contributes approximately 1.7 percentage points to the quarterly standard deviation of stock returns and 12 basis points to 10-year nominal Treasury yield volatility.
■ A calibrated New Keynesian model shows MPR accounts for 40 basis points of the 10-year nominal term premium and 120 basis points of the equity risk premium.
■ During the post-2021 tightening, MPR shocks added 35 basis points to the 10-year real term premium, raised the VIX by 5 points, and reduced equity prices by 4%.

Why it matters

This paper provides rates and equity strategists with a framework to isolate reaction-function uncertainty from broader macroeconomic risks. By showing that monetary-policy risk depresses equity valuations and drives real term premia while lowering inflation risk premia, the model offers a quantitative benchmark for asset price reactions during policy shifts. Rates strategists can apply these insights to decompose term premium movements around FOMC communications and effective lower bound constraints.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses on US monetary policy, evaluating FOMC communications, Survey of Primary Dealers data, and US Treasury and equity market data from 1994 to 2026.

Developed marketsBoard of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 30 Oct 2026

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

Hoopes, Charlotte; Zer, Ilknur; Uysal, Pinar

This paper examines whether sovereign risk still affects banks a decade after Europe began reforming its financial architecture. Using daily bank and sovereign credit default swap spreads alongside supervisory data on domestic sovereign portfolios, the authors show that the sovereign-bank relationship remains meaningful but conditional. Banks with larger sovereign holdings respond more sharply to home-sovereign risk, particularly for mark-to-market exposures compared to amortized-cost holdings. Transmission is stronger for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks during elevated uncertainty and when fiscal capacity is low. Policy reforms reduced this link but did not eliminate it.

Key takeaways

■ The sample covers 34 European banks across 12 countries from October 2014 to December 2025.
■ Banks with larger domestic sovereign portfolios respond more sharply to home-sovereign risk, with amplification substantially stronger for mark-to-market than amortized-cost exposures.
■ Sovereign-to-bank transmission is stronger for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks, as well as during periods of elevated market uncertainty.

Why it matters

This study informs sovereign debt and bank credit research in European financial markets. Rates and credit strategists assessing peripheral versus core sovereign spreads or bank unsecured debt and CDS pricing can use the findings to differentiate bank vulnerability based on asset-side accounting treatment (mark-to-market versus amortized-cost holdings) and balance-sheet capitalization. It directly speaks to the effectiveness of post-crisis European institutional reforms and the ongoing sovereign-bank nexus.

Classified developed markets. The paper focuses exclusively on post-crisis Europe and euro-area member states.

Developed marketsDeutsche Bundesbank, 30 Oct 2026

What volatility reveals

Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

This paper investigates why the April 2025 U.S. tariff announcements produced an unusual combination of dollar depreciation and rising long-term U.S. Treasury yields. Using a structural vector autoregression model estimated on daily data from January 2008 to May 2025, the authors exploit time-varying volatility and excess kurtosis to identify macroeconomic and financial shocks without relying on event restrictions. The findings show that while the initial market reaction mirrored a conventional safe-haven shock, subsequent asset price dynamics were driven by orthogonal forces including U.S. institutional credibility, Treasury market intermediation, and asset convenience values.

Key takeaways

■ The April 2025 tariff announcement triggered a conventional safe-haven shock in equities, but this mechanism fails to explain the subsequent joint behavior of exchange rates and long-term Treasury yields.
■ Dollar depreciation and rising long-term Treasury yields are primarily associated with a broad repricing of U.S. dollar assets consistent with a deterioration in U.S. institutional credibility.
■ A distinct Treasury market intermediation shock accounts for impairments in swap spreads and Treasury convenience values, separating broader safe-asset demand from intermediation capacity.

Why it matters

This study informs fixed income, foreign exchange, and rates strategists analyzing the transmission of trade policy shocks and the fragmentation of U.S. international privilege. By separating safe-haven demand from institutional credibility and Treasury market intermediation using daily data from 2008 to 2025, the research provides a framework for understanding why safe-haven currencies and sovereign yields may decouple during policy-driven market stress.

Classified developed markets. The paper focuses specifically on United States tariff announcements, U.S. Treasury yields, the U.S. dollar, and G10 sovereign bond dynamics.

TransversalEuropean Central Bank, 27 Oct 2026

The Tipping Point: Interest Rates and Financial Stability

Davide Porcellacchia

This ECB working paper examines how interest rate changes affect bank stability using a standard bank-run model. When interest rates rise, they exert two opposing pressures on banks: they decrease the market value of long-term assets, but they also widen interest margins earned from insuring depositors' liquidity risk. The author characterizes the specific conditions under which asset devaluation outweighs margin expansion, and identifies the interest rate level that triggers instability. Quantification suggests that a negative effective maturity mismatch can create a lower bound on interest rates where instability risks emerge.

Key takeaways

■ Higher interest rates reduce long-term asset values while simultaneously widening bank interest margins.
■ Interest-rate risk is captured by a bank's maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of its deposits.
■ Quantification indicates a potential negative effective maturity mismatch, turning the instability threshold into a lower bound on interest rates.

Why it matters

This paper informs research on banking sector stability, monetary policy transmission, and net interest margin dynamics. Rates and credit strategists assessing bank vulnerabilities to shifting yield curves can use the framework's focus on maturity mismatch and deposit duration. While theoretical, the findings directly relate to ongoing debates on how rapid monetary tightening cycles impact institutional funding stability and asset valuations across the banking sector.

Classified transversal. The paper uses a theoretical bank-run model without naming specific countries, central banks, or geographic datasets in the abstract.

Developed marketsBanco de España, 27 Oct 2026

Measuring Euro-Area Inflation Risk with Options: Market-Implied Distribution and Balance of Risks

Diego Bonelli

This Banco de España working paper extracts market-implied inflation risk distributions for the euro area using options data, specifically caps and floors. By comparing a standard normal model against two non-normal alternatives—the SABR smile and a nonparametric estimator—the author demonstrates that options markets price in skewed and fat-tailed inflation outcomes that normal models miss. The study constructs forward distributions via a copula and introduces a balance-of-risks index, revealing that market-implied risk assessments shift quickly at short horizons while remaining relatively stable further out, and generally run lower than survey-based professional forecasts.

Key takeaways

■ Non-normal models (SABR and nonparametric) reveal skewed and fat-tailed euro-area inflation risk distributions from option prices.
■ The option-implied balance-of-risks index varies sharply over short horizons but moves more slowly over longer horizons.
■ Option-implied balance of risks sits generally lower than corresponding measures from professional forecasters on matched dates.

Why it matters

This paper is relevant for rates and inflation derivatives strategists pricing euro-area caps, floors, and inflation-linked swaps. By demonstrating that options assign higher probability to extreme outcomes than standard normal benchmarks, the findings help risk managers better calibrate volatility smiles and tail-risk hedging. The comparison between market-implied pricing and professional forecaster surveys also offers insight into inflation risk premia and market sentiment divergences.

Classified developed markets. The paper explicitly studies euro-area inflation risk using data from interest rate options.

Developed marketsEuropean Central Bank, 26 Oct 2026

Derivative margin calls: A new driver of MMF flows

Linda Rousová, Dilyara Salakhova, Germán Villegas-Bauer

During the March 2020 market turmoil, euro area money market funds experienced severe redemptions, losing nearly eight percent of their assets under management. New research from the European Central Bank demonstrates that margin calls on derivative contracts acted as a major driver of these outflows. Because financial intermediaries utilize money market funds for liquidity management, sudden cash demands from derivative positions forced these entities to pull funds from the market. This mechanism accounted for approximately forty-five percent of aggregate outflows from euro-denominated money market funds during the peak of the COVID-19 shock.

Key takeaways

■ Derivative margin calls faced by non-bank financial intermediaries drove around 45% of aggregate outflows from euro-denominated money market funds between February and April 2020.
■ Financial intermediaries use money market funds as cash management vehicles, creating a direct transmission channel from derivative markets to fund liquidity.
■ The COVID-19 market turmoil triggered euro area money market fund outflows reaching nearly 8% of assets under management.

Why it matters

This paper informs research on short-term funding markets, liquidity risk, and non-bank financial intermediation. Rates and liquidity strategists monitoring euro area money market funds can incorporate derivative margin call dynamics into models tracking systemic shock propagation. The findings highlight structural linkages between derivatives clearing obligations and cash management vehicles that are relevant for regulatory debates on margin requirements and MMF resilience.

Classified developed markets. The paper exclusively analyzes data from the euro area and focuses on euro-denominated money market funds and regional non-bank financial intermediaries.

TransversalBanca d'Italia, 22 Oct 2026

The Reaction of Financial Markets to the Releases of DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K3: A Critical Analysis

Marco Taboga

This paper examines how financial markets reacted to the releases of Chinese artificial intelligence models DeepSeek R1 in January 2025 and Kimi K3 in July 2026. While both models approached the capability frontier, their economic signals differed. DeepSeek combined open weights with unusually low training and inference costs, triggering a sell-off in semiconductor and data-centre shares. Kimi K3 was priced at frontier levels and heavily impacted rival Chinese labs. The study argues that open-weight models redistribute rents across the value chain rather than eliminating them, protected by incumbent complementary assets.

Key takeaways

■ DeepSeek R1's January 2025 release triggered large declines in semiconductor-related firms, including a 17.0% drop in NVIDIA and a 9.1% drop in the SOX index.
■ Kimi K3's July 2026 release produced smaller semiconductor drops but steep losses for rival Chinese labs, with Zhipu AI falling 35.1% and MiniMax Group falling 17.1%.
■ OpenRouter weekly token usage surged from 0.53 trillion in January 2025 to 58.00 trillion in July 2026, while the top four US laboratories' share fell from 64% to 23%.

Why it matters

This paper informs equity strategists covering technology hardware, semiconductors, and software by assessing the commercial threat posed by Chinese frontier models. It provides a structured framework for analyzing how open-weight releases redistribute value across the AI stack rather than destroying it entirely, highlighting the protective role of customer switching costs, distribution, and proprietary data moats.

Classified transversal. The paper investigates a global technology market involving competition and market reactions across both the United States and China.

Emerging marketsBank for International Settlements (BIS), 16 Oct 2026

Offshore forwards and exchange rate transmission in emerging markets

Gerardo Ferrara, Andreas Schrimpf, Ganesh Viswanath-Natraj, Zijie Wang

This study examines how offshore non-deliverable forward markets transmit exchange-rate pressures to domestic emerging-market currency currencies. Using transaction-level data on the universe of centrally cleared non-deliverable forwards for ten emerging-market currencies from 2013 to 2025, the authors demonstrate that offshore order flow moves both offshore forward and domestic spot rates while widening the offshore-onshore forward spread. The authors develop an equilibrium model where capital controls segment offshore and onshore markets, and dealers hedge offshore inventory through domestic spot markets.

Key takeaways

■ A $1 billion long-USD order flow depreciates the domestic spot rate by about 10 basis points and the offshore NDF rate by about 18 basis points.
■ The dataset covers the universe of centrally cleared NDFs at LCH ForexClear for ten emerging-market currencies over the 2013–2025 period.
■ The spot price impact of offshore order flow increases when primary dealer balance sheets are tight.

Why it matters

This research informs strategists and macro economists tracking emerging-market exchange rates, capital controls, and cross-border currency transmission. The findings provide empirical estimates of how offshore order imbalances and dealer balance-sheet constraints affect spot rates and forward spreads across ten emerging-market currency pairs.

Classified emerging markets. The paper exclusively studies ten emerging-market currency pairs and their offshore non-deliverable forward markets.
