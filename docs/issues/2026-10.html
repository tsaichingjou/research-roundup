Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices

King, Thomas B.; Feldman, Corey

This paper quantifies how uncertainty regarding the central bank's reaction function—termed monetary-policy risk (MPR)—impacts equilibrium asset pricing. Analyzing US financial data from 1994 to 2026, the authors show that asset price volatility is significantly higher outside FOMC blackout periods, with MPR contributing 1.7 percentage points to quarterly equity volatility and 12 basis points to 10-year Treasury yield volatility. A calibrated non-linear New Keynesian model demonstrates that MPR contributes roughly 40 basis points to the average 10-year nominal term premium and 120 basis points to the equity premium, with asset-pricing consequences further magnified at the effective lower bound.

Key takeaways

■ Monetary-policy risk contributes approximately 1.7 percentage points to the quarterly standard deviation of stock returns and 12 basis points to 10-year nominal Treasury yield volatility.
■ A calibrated New Keynesian model shows MPR accounts for 40 basis points of the 10-year nominal term premium and 120 basis points of the equity risk premium.
■ During the post-2021 tightening, MPR shocks added 35 basis points to the 10-year real term premium, raised the VIX by 5 points, and reduced equity prices by 4%.

Why it matters

This paper provides rates and equity strategists with a framework to isolate reaction-function uncertainty from broader macroeconomic risks. By showing that monetary-policy risk depresses equity valuations and drives real term premia while lowering inflation risk premia, the model offers a quantitative benchmark for asset price reactions during policy shifts. Rates strategists can apply these insights to decompose term premium movements around FOMC communications and effective lower bound constraints.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses on US monetary policy, evaluating FOMC communications, Survey of Primary Dealers data, and US Treasury and equity market data from 1994 to 2026.

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

Hoopes, Charlotte; Zer, Ilknur; Uysal, Pinar

This paper examines whether sovereign credit risk continues to transmit to European banks following post-crisis institutional reforms. Analyzing daily credit default swap (CDS) spreads and supervisory balance-sheet data for 34 European banks across 12 countries from 2014 to 2025, the authors find that the sovereign-bank relationship remains economically meaningful. Banks with larger domestic sovereign portfolios are more sensitive to home-sovereign risk, particularly for holdings measured at mark-to-market (MTM) rather than amortized cost. Transmission is stronger for peripheral, less-capitalized, and wholesale-funded banks, during periods of market uncertainty, and in countries with elevated public debt burdens.

Key takeaways

■ Evaluating 34 banks across 12 European countries from 2014 to 2025, sovereign credit default swap increases transmit more strongly to banks holding larger domestic sovereign debt portfolios.
■ A shift from the 10th to 90th percentile of mark-to-market sovereign exposure raises risk pass-through by 2.4 percentage points, compared to 1.7 percentage points for amortized-cost holdings.
■ Transmission is amplified for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks, as well as under higher sovereign debt-to-GDP ratios and during periods of elevated market uncertainty.

Why it matters

For rates and credit strategists, the study shows that post-crisis reforms reduced but did not eliminate sovereign-bank feedback loops in Europe. Total sovereign bond holdings alone understate risk; accounting classification matters significantly because mark-to-market holdings accelerate risk transmission to bank CDS and equity valuations. Analysts tracking sovereign spreads in peripheral countries should account for domestic bank balance-sheet composition, capital buffers, and reliance on wholesale funding when assessing systemic vulnerability during market stress.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses exclusively on European economies, examining data from 34 banks across 12 European countries including euro-area core and peripheral nations.

What volatility reveals

Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

This paper examines financial market reactions to the April 2025 U.S. tariff announcements, focusing on the unusual combination of U.S. dollar depreciation and rising long-term U.S. Treasury yields. Using a VAR model identified through time-varying stochastic volatility and Student-t shock distributions on daily data from January 2008 to May 2025, the authors decompose market dynamics without event or exclusion restrictions. They find that while initial equity declines reflected a standard safe-haven shock, subsequent market movements were driven by distinct orthogonal disturbances: a U.S. institutional credibility shock that depreciated the dollar and raised yields, and a Treasury intermediation shock that impaired swap spreads.

Key takeaways

■ Traditional safe-haven shocks explain initial equity drops and VIX spikes during April 2025, but fail to account for persistent U.S. dollar depreciation and rising long-term Treasury yields.
■ The post-announcement dollar depreciation and yield increases were driven by a U.S. institutional credibility shock that repriced dollar assets and raised one-year inflation swap expectations.
■ Using daily data from 2008 to 2025, a separate Treasury market intermediation shock explains the narrowing of 10-year U.S. swap spreads and declines in Treasury convenience yields.

Why it matters

This paper informs FX, rates, and cross-asset strategists analyzing trade policy shocks and reserve-currency dynamics. It demonstrates that U.S. trade announcements do not mechanically induce traditional safe-haven inflows into the dollar and U.S. Treasuries. By isolating separate shocks to U.S. institutional credibility and Treasury market intermediation, strategists can better model scenarios where trade policy announcements weaken the dollar, push up U.S. long-term yields and term premia, and impair Treasury dealer balance-sheet capacity.

Classified developed markets. The empirical study focuses entirely on advanced economy financial assets, analyzing U.S. Treasuries, the U.S. dollar, euro area government bond yields, and U.S. equity indexes.

TransversalEuropean Central Bank, 27 Oct 2026

The Tipping Point: Interest Rates and Financial Stability

Davide Porcellacchia

This paper examines how interest rate changes affect bank stability using a theoretical bank-run framework. Higher interest rates exert two opposing pressures on banks: they decrease the value of long-term assets while increasing net interest margins earned on deposit insurance provision. The net impact depends on a bank's maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of deposits. Under certain conditions, such as a negative effective maturity mismatch, the critical threshold for bank instability acts as a lower bound on interest rates. This indicates that excessively low interest rates, rather than high rates, may present the primary risk to financial stability in specific structural settings.

Key takeaways

■ Interest rates affect bank stability via two opposing mechanisms: reducing long-term asset values while expanding net interest margins derived from liquidity-risk insurance.
■ The dominant effect is determined by a bank's maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of its deposit base.
■ Quantitative findings highlight that a negative effective maturity mismatch can turn the financial stability tipping point into a lower bound on policy interest rates.

Why it matters

This research informs debates on central bank policy rate paths, bank equity valuations, and financial stability monitoring. Financial strategists and rates analysts can apply the concept of effective maturity mismatch—factoring in deposit duration—to assess bank balance sheet vulnerability across different rate regimes. It suggests that prolonged low interest rate environments may pose greater structural risks to banking solvency than rate hikes when deposit stickiness provides substantial duration cushioning.

Classified transversal. The paper develops a theoretical bank-run model and quantitative exercise without restricting its framework or data to a specific country or market dataset.

Measuring Euro-Area Inflation Risk with Options: Market-Implied Distribution and Balance of Risks

Diego Bonelli

This study extracts market-implied probability distributions for euro-area inflation using options contracts, specifically inflation caps and floors. Comparing non-normal modeling approaches—the SABR model and a nonparametric estimator—against a normal benchmark, the author constructs forward distributions using a copula. The non-normal frameworks show that inflation distributions are skewed with fat tails, pricing higher probabilities of extreme inflation outcomes than standard normal models. A derived balance-of-risks index displays significant variation over short horizons but stays more stable over longer horizons. Finally, the option-implied balance of risks is generally lower than comparable expectations recorded in surveys of professional forecasters.

Key takeaways

■ Non-normal models (SABR and nonparametric) reveal that euro-area inflation option distributions are skewed and fat-tailed, pricing higher probabilities of extreme outcomes than normal benchmarks.
■ The option-implied balance-of-risks index exhibits sharp variations over short-term horizons, but shows more constrained movement over longer horizons.
■ Market option-implied balance of inflation risks is generally lower on matched dates compared to the balance of risks reported by professional forecasters.

Why it matters

This research provides euro-area inflation and rates strategists with a framework for evaluating tail risks and skewness priced into inflation derivatives. By demonstrating that normal models underestimate extreme inflation outcomes, the paper highlights the necessity of using non-normal pricing models like SABR when extracting market expectations. Additionally, the systematic divergence between option-implied risk measures and survey-based professional forecasts offers a baseline for tracking shifts in risk premia versus consensus surveys over different horizon lengths.

Classified developed markets. The paper specifically analyzes euro-area inflation expectations derived from euro-area inflation caps and floors.

Developed marketsEuropean Central Bank, 26 Oct 2026

Derivative margin calls: A new driver of MMF flows

Linda Rousová, Dilyara Salakhova, Germán Villegas-Bauer

During the COVID-19 market turmoil in early 2020, euro area money market funds (MMFs) experienced redemptions reaching nearly 8% of assets under management. Analyzing granular transaction data, this study identifies a novel shock transmission channel: non-bank financial intermediaries use MMFs as cash management vehicles. When market volatility triggers derivative margin calls, these institutions redeem MMF holdings to fulfill cash demands. The authors find that margin calls faced by euro area non-bank financial intermediaries accounted for roughly 45% of total outflows from euro-denominated euro area MMFs between February and April 2020, highlighting strong linkages between derivatives markets and fund flows.

Key takeaways

■ Euro area money market funds experienced outflows of nearly 8% of assets under management during the COVID-19 market shock between February and April 2020.
■ Derivative margin calls on euro area non-bank financial intermediaries drove approximately 45% of aggregate outflows from EUR-denominated money market funds in early 2020.
■ Non-bank financial intermediaries use money market funds as cash management vehicles, creating a direct transmission channel from derivative market shocks to fund redemptions.

Why it matters

This paper highlights systemic linkages between derivative margin requirements and short-term money market fund liquidity in the euro area. It informs central bank policy debates regarding non-bank financial intermediation, liquidity buffers, and MMF structural reform. Money market and rates strategists can use these empirical insights to better anticipate fund redemption pressures and liquidity friction during periods of sharp derivative market repricing and heightened margin calls.

Classified developed markets. The paper relies exclusively on data from euro area non-bank financial intermediaries and EUR-denominated euro area money market funds.

The Reaction of Financial Markets to the Releases of DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K3: A Critical Analysis

Marco Taboga

This paper compares global equity market reactions to the releases of Chinese AI models DeepSeek R1 in January 2025 and Kimi K3 in July 2026. DeepSeek R1 combined open weights with low reported training costs, triggering sharp sell-offs in compute supply chain equities (NVIDIA -17.0%, SOX -9.1%) as investors marked down expected hardware demand. Conversely, Kimi K3 entered at near-frontier prices, concentrating losses among competing pure-play AI labs (Zhipu AI -35.1%, MiniMax -17.1%). The author argues open-weight models redistribute economic rents across the value chain rather than eliminating them, with incumbents shielded by complementary assets such as distribution, system integration, and trust.

Key takeaways

■ DeepSeek R1 (Jan 2025) drove hardware supply chain sell-offs (NVIDIA -17.0%, SOX -9.1%) by signaling low compute costs, while Kimi K3 (Jul 2026) primarily hit rival AI labs (Zhipu AI -35.1%).
■ OpenRouter total token usage surged from 0.53T to 58T per week between Jan 2025 and Jul 2026, while top US labs expanded absolute token volume nearly 40-fold (0.34T to 13.19T).
■ Incumbent AI providers preserve market power through complementary assets—including product bundling, switching costs, serving capacity, enterprise trust, and proprietary data moats—rather than raw model weights alone.

Why it matters

Relevant for tech equity strategists, credit analysts, and macro economists evaluating AI hardware demand and capital expenditure sustainability. By demonstrating that open-weight model releases reallocate rents across the software stack rather than destroying aggregate tech value, the study helps quantify cross-sectional risks between chipmakers, specialized AI labs, and diversified tech incumbents. Strategists can use the framework to differentiate between model-layer price shocks and compute-demand shifts when assessing technology sector earnings trajectories.

Classified transversal. The paper examines equity market reactions across both advanced markets (US equity indices and semiconductor incumbents) and emerging markets (Chinese AI laboratories and conglomerates).

Offshore forwards and exchange rate transmission in emerging markets

Gerardo Ferrara, Andreas Schrimpf, Ganesh Viswanath-Natraj, Zijie Wang

This paper examines how trading activity in offshore non-deliverable forward (NDF) markets transmits exchange-rate pressure to domestic currency markets in emerging economies. Utilizing transaction-level clearing data for ten emerging-market currencies from 2013 to 2025, the authors demonstrate that offshore order flow moves both offshore forward and domestic spot exchange rates while widening the offshore-onshore forward spread. The transmission occurs primarily because dealers holding NDF positions hedge their inventory risk directly in domestic spot markets. Consequently, capital controls and dealer balance-sheet constraints dictate the extent to which offshore speculative demand drives onshore exchange-rate volatility.

Key takeaways

■ A $1 billion long-USD NDF order flow depreciates domestic spot rates by ~10 basis points and offshore NDF rates by ~18 basis points across 10 EM currencies (2013–2025).
■ Spot price impact is state-dependent, dropping from 17.8 basis points when dealer gross FX positions are one standard deviation below mean to 7.1 basis points when above.
■ Offshore order flow transmits to domestic spot rates via dealer hedging, while balance-sheet costs and capital controls determine the magnitude of the offshore–onshore spread.

Why it matters

This research provides actionable insights for EM FX strategists, risk managers, and macro investors tracking foreign exchange microstructure under capital controls. By establishing how offshore NDF order flow affects domestic spot markets across ten major EM currencies, the study equips research teams to model currency spillovers and cross-market spreads. Strategists can apply these metrics to evaluate dealer balance-sheet capacity, anticipate spot volatility, and assess regulatory changes governing domestic bank access to offshore NDF trading.

Classified emerging markets. The study focuses exclusively on price discovery, capital controls, and exchange-rate dynamics across ten emerging-market currencies.
