Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices

King, Thomas B.; Feldman, Corey

This paper investigates how uncertainty regarding the conduct of monetary policy—termed monetary-policy risk—affects asset prices and macroeconomic conditions. Using a New Keynesian model and empirical data from 1994 to 2026, the authors demonstrate that policy communication and uncertainty act as independent pricing factors. Specifically, temporary increases in this risk cause stock and bond prices to decline, raise real and nominal term premia, and generate macroeconomic weakness. The effective lower bound on interest rates amplifies these asset-pricing consequences.

Key takeaways

■ Monetary-policy risk contributes about 1.7 percentage points to the quarterly standard deviation of stock prices and 12 basis points to the 10-year nominal yield in daily data from 1994 to 2026.
■ A New Keynesian model shows that monetary-policy risk accounts for about 40 basis points of the average nominal 10-year term premium and 120 basis points of the equity premium.
■ Following the rapid tightening of policy in 2022, monetary-policy risk shocks added about 35 basis points to the 10-year real term premium and caused a cumulative equity drop of about 4 percent.

Why it matters

This research provides strategists and rates or equity desks with a structural framework to price monetary-policy uncertainty into yield curves and equity risk premia. It explains why asset prices react significantly to communication and interest-rate uncertainty around FOMC announcements, and demonstrates how proximity to the effective lower bound magnifies these effects. The findings help analysts deconstruct movements in term premia and inflation compensation during tightening cycles.

Classified developed markets. The study's empirical analysis focuses on United States financial data, primary dealer surveys, and Federal Open Market Committee communications.

Developed marketsFederal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, 18 Sep 2026

Risk Appetite and Monetary Transmission

Schrimpf, Andreas; Schmeling, Maik; Bauer, Michael D.

Economists construct a new high-frequency measure of risk appetite shifts around Federal Open Market Committee meetings using eleven risk-sensitive indicators. The study shows that interest-rate surprises explain only about one-fifth of the variation in risk appetite, indicating that most policy-induced changes in risk asset prices are orthogonal to the expected rate path. Using a proxy structural vector autoregression with external instruments, the authors find that risk appetite shocks have large, persistent contractionary effects on output and prices while raising unemployment, whereas risk-free rate shocks have small and imprecisely estimated effects.

Key takeaways

■ Interest-rate surprises explain only about one-fifth of the variation in risk appetite around Federal Open Market Committee meetings from January 1996 to September 2025.
■ Risk appetite shocks account for 10% to 40% of the unconditional forecast-error variance of industrial production, consumer prices, and unemployment in the main proxy structural vector autoregression specification.
■ Conventional monetary policy shocks identified using risk-free rate surprises alone explain at most 5% of the unconditional forecast-error variance in the joint identification scheme.

Why it matters

This paper informs rates, FX, and equity strategists by challenging the view that monetary transmission operates solely through risk-free rate expectations. It demonstrates that policy communication drives asset prices primarily through risk appetite and risk premia. Quantitative and macro-oriented desks can incorporate the joint identification framework to better capture the macroeconomic impacts of central bank announcements, which are often missed when relying exclusively on interest-rate surprises.

Classified developed markets. The study uses data from United States Federal Open Market Committee meetings and analyzes U.S. macroeconomic variables such as industrial production, consumer prices, and unemployment.

Developed marketsAmerican Economic Review, 1 Nov 2026

The Inflation Attention Threshold and Inflation Surges

Oliver Pfäuti

This paper investigates how public attention to inflation shifts depending on the inflation rate and how these changes impact macroeconomic dynamics. Using survey and time-series data from the United States, the author identifies an inflation attention threshold at about 4 percent, above which attention roughly doubles. The study demonstrates that when attention is high, adverse supply shocks have stronger and more persistent effects on inflation. Incorporating this threshold into a structural model helps explain inflation surges, the co-movement between inflation and expectations, and a prolonged disinflation phase.

Key takeaways

■ The estimated inflation attention threshold is about 4 percent, with public attention roughly doubling when inflation exceeds this level based on U.S. survey data from 1978 to 2024.
■ Adverse supply shocks have significantly stronger and more persistent effects on inflation during times of high attention.
■ Attention changes provide a joint explanation for inflation surges, co-movement with inflation expectations, and a slow disinflation phase.

Why it matters

This research is relevant for fixed income and FX strategists analyzing inflation persistence, central bank reaction functions, and the transmission of supply shocks. The finding that inflation expectations become more sensitive and persistent once inflation breaches a 4 percent threshold helps explain why central banks face a difficult 'last mile' of disinflation and why standard policy rules may require hawkish adjustments to prevent de-anchoring risks.

Classified developed markets. The study relies on U.S. survey and macroeconomic data from 1978 to 2024 and focuses its empirical estimations on the United States.

Developed marketsBoard of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 30 Oct 2026

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

Hoopes, Charlotte; Zer, Ilknur; Uysal, Pinar

This paper examines whether sovereign risk continues to affect European banks a decade after the sovereign debt crisis and subsequent institutional reforms. Using daily credit default swap spreads and supervisory data from 2014 to 2025 across 34 European banks in 12 countries, the authors show that the sovereign-bank link remains economically meaningful but highly conditional. Transmission is stronger for banks with larger domestic sovereign holdings, particularly mark-to-market exposures rather than amortized-cost holdings. Furthermore, vulnerability is pronounced for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks during periods of high uncertainty.

Key takeaways

■ The sample covers 34 European banks across 12 countries from October 2014 to December 2025.
■ Banks with larger domestic sovereign holdings respond more sharply to home-sovereign risk, with valuation-loss amplification substantially stronger for mark-to-market exposures than amortized-cost exposures.
■ Sovereign-to-bank transmission is stronger for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks, during elevated market uncertainty, and when home sovereigns have less fiscal capacity.

Why it matters

This study informs credit, sovereign, and rates strategists monitoring euro-area peripheral debt and bank balance-sheet risks. It highlights that aggregate sovereign exposure metrics can be misleading unless adjusted for accounting treatment (mark-to-market versus amortized cost) and bank capitalization. The findings demonstrate that post-reform policy interventions have reduced but not eliminated the sovereign-bank feedback loop, leaving vulnerabilities concentrated in specific institutions and fiscal environments.

Classified developed markets. The paper relies entirely on European banking supervisory data and credit default swap spreads spanning 12 European countries.

Developed marketsDeutsche Bundesbank, 30 Oct 2026

What volatility reveals

Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

This Deutsche Bundesbank paper investigates why the U.S. dollar depreciated while long-term U.S. Treasury yields rose following the April 2025 U.S. tariff announcements. Analyzing high-frequency asset pricing data through a heteroskedastic VAR model, the authors find that the episode cannot be explained as a single tariff shock. Instead, the market reaction reflects the interaction of multiple orthogonal forces, combining an initial conventional safe-haven shock with subsequent shocks driven by U.S. institutional credibility, Treasury-market intermediation, and changes in the convenience value of dollar-denominated safe assets.

Key takeaways

■ The April 2025 tariff announcements activated an unusual combination of dollar depreciation and higher long-term U.S. Treasury yields, unlike the August 2019 trade war.
■ Agnostic econometric identification shows the initial market response reflects a standard global risk-off safe-haven shock.
■ Subsequent asset price dynamics were driven primarily by a U.S. Institutional Credibility shock, which raised Treasury risk premia and weakened the currency.

Why it matters

This research provides a framework for FX and rates strategists analyzing regimes where traditional safe-haven correlations break down. It informs trading desks monitoring U.S. term spreads, dollar hegemony, and the market pricing of institutional and policy credibility following trade policy shifts. The findings highlight that tariff shocks can trigger distinct, competing macro-financial mechanisms rather than a uniform risk-off response.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses exclusively on U.S. financial markets, U.S. Treasury yields, the U.S. dollar, and U.S. trade policy announcements.

TransversalEuropean Central Bank, 27 Oct 2026

The Tipping Point: Interest Rates and Financial Stability

Davide Porcellacchia

This ECB working paper examines how interest rate changes affect bank stability using a standard bank-run model. Banks face two opposing forces as rates rise: the market value of their long-term assets falls, but their interest margins widen because of how they insure depositors against liquidity risk. The author derives conditions to determine which effect dominates, showing that interest-rate risk depends on maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of deposits. The paper also calculates the specific interest rate level that triggers bank instability, suggesting that a negative effective maturity mismatch can create a lower bound on rates.

Key takeaways

■ Higher interest rates reduce the value of banks' long-term assets while simultaneously widening their interest margins.
■ Interest-rate risk is captured by a bank's maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of its deposits.
■ Quantification indicates that a negative effective maturity mismatch can establish a lower bound on interest rates to prevent instability.

Why it matters

This paper informs bank equity and fixed income strategists analyzing the transmission of monetary policy to the banking sector. The theoretical framework links interest rate levels directly to bank run vulnerabilities via asset valuation and net interest margin dynamics. Rates and credit strategists monitoring financial stability risks can apply the concept of adjusted maturity mismatch to assess how changes in policy rates affect systemic stability thresholds across lending institutions.

Classified transversal. The abstract outlines a theoretical bank-run model and general mechanisms without referencing specific countries, regions, or empirical data.

Measuring Euro-Area Inflation Risk with Options: Market-Implied Distribution and Balance of Risks

Diego Bonelli

This Banco de España working paper extracts market-implied risk-neutral distributions for euro-area inflation from options data, specifically caps and floors. By applying parametric SABR smile models and nonparametric estimators rather than a standard normal benchmark, the author captures skewed and fat-tailed inflation risks. The study constructs forward distributions using a copula and aggregates these via a balance-of-risks index. Findings indicate that market-implied risks fluctuate significantly over short horizons and generally sit below comparable measures reported by professional forecasters on matched dates.

Key takeaways

■ Non-normal models (SABR and nonparametric) reveal skewed and fat-tailed euro-area inflation distributions, whereas a normal benchmark understates extreme outcomes.
■ The option-implied balance-of-risks index varies sharply at short horizons but remains relatively stable over longer horizons.
■ Market-implied balance of risks tends to register lower readings than corresponding surveys of professional forecasters on identical dates.

Why it matters

This research provides rates and inflation options desks with alternative methodologies for pricing tail risk and assessing market pricing versus survey expectations in the euro area. By highlighting discrepancies between options-derived risk measures and professional forecasts, the paper assists strategists in evaluating inflation risk premia and sentiment extremes in the derivatives market, directly informing inflation-linked swap and swaption trading strategies.

Classified developed markets. The paper explicitly examines euro-area inflation distributions using data from caps and floors, which directly ties its empirical setting to developed markets.

Emerging marketsBank for International Settlements (BIS), 16 Oct 2026

Offshore forwards and exchange rate transmission in emerging markets

Gerardo Ferrara, Andreas Schrimpf, Ganesh Viswanath-Natraj, Zijie Wang

This Bank for International Settlements working paper investigates how offshore non-deliverable forward markets transmit exchange rate pressures to domestic emerging market currency spots. Using transaction-level data for ten currencies from 2014 to 2025, the authors examine price formation and the role of capital controls and bank intermediation. They develop a model highlighting offshore demand and dealer intermediation costs. Four regulatory episodes in China, Malaysia, and India illustrate how access restrictions and speculative activity impact offshore-onshore price spreads and domestic spot rates.

Key takeaways

■ A $1 billion increase in offshore dollar demand moves the onshore exchange rate by about 10 basis points and the offshore forward rate by about 18 basis points within minutes.
■ The analysis uses transaction-level data on non-deliverable forward trades cleared through LCH ForexClear for 10 emerging market currencies from 2014 to 2025.
■ Policy episodes in China, Malaysia, and India demonstrate that the transmission of offshore trading pressure to domestic markets depends on how tightly banks' cross-market activity is regulated.

Why it matters

This paper is relevant for emerging market foreign exchange strategists and rates desks tracking currency transmission and capital controls. By providing transaction-level evidence on order flow and the offshore-onshore forward spread, it offers insights into how offshore speculative activity impacts domestic spot exchange rates under varying regulatory frameworks and dealer balance sheet constraints.

Classified emerging markets. The paper focuses specifically on emerging market currencies, utilizing transaction-level data for ten EM economies and examining policy episodes in China, Malaysia, and India.

How this issue was made

An automated agent screened 719 new publications from 54 sources (2 Oct 2026 to 2 Nov 2026). 671 with a usable abstract were scored for market relevance by a language model, and the 8 above were selected, with at most two papers per source.

For 46 papers whose feed carried no abstract, a stand-in was recovered before screening: from OpenAlex, the publisher's page, or the opening of the paper's own text.
