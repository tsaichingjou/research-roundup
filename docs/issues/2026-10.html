Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices

King, Thomas B.; Feldman, Corey

This paper quantifies how uncertainty about monetary policy affects asset prices and the broader economy. Asset prices, including nominal and real bond yields, stocks, and option-implied volatility, fluctuate more during FOMC communication periods than during blackout windows. Using a New Keynesian framework with variable policy rules, the authors demonstrate that monetary policy risk increases excess returns on equities and nominal bonds while driving a large share of the risk premium on inflation-indexed securities. Spikes in policy uncertainty depress stock and bond valuations, lower inflation-risk premia, and induce economic weakness similar to aggregate demand shocks, with heightened effects during post-2022 policy tightening.

Key takeaways

■ Nominal yields, real yields, equities, and volatility move significantly more on FOMC communication days than during blackout periods.
■ Monetary policy uncertainty increases excess returns for equities and nominal bonds and accounts for most of the inflation-indexed bond risk premium.
■ Temporary increases in policy risk depress asset prices, shrink inflation-risk premia, and induce macroeconomic weakness similar to aggregate demand shocks.

Why it matters

This study provides rates and equity strategists with a structural framework to evaluate how central bank uncertainty affects asset valuations and risk premia. It is directly relevant for inflation-linked bond trading, as policy risk explains a major portion of TIPS risk premia. Macro strategists can use the paper's calibrated model to assess how shifting policy predictability—such as during the Fed's 2022 tightening cycle or near the effective lower bound—shapes market risk premia and macroeconomic trajectories.

Classified developed markets. The analysis focuses on empirical evidence from FOMC announcements, blackout periods, and the Fed's 2022 policy tightening, grounding the study in the United States.

Developed marketsFederal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, 18 Sep 2026

Risk Appetite and Monetary Transmission

Schrimpf, Andreas; Schmeling, Maik; Bauer, Michael D.

This paper constructs a high-frequency measure of shifts in risk appetite around Federal Reserve policy meetings. The authors find that conventional interest-rate surprises account for only about 20% of the variation in risk appetite caused by monetary policy, as Federal Reserve communications shift risk asset prices independently of the expected path of interest rates. Using a structural econometric model, they show that monetary policy affects the broader economy—such as output, employment, and inflation—primarily through changes in risk appetite rather than through the risk-free rate path alone. Traditional empirical estimates that rely solely on rate surprises miss these principal transmission channels.

Key takeaways

■ Interest-rate surprises account for only about 20% of the policy-driven variation in risk appetite around FOMC meetings.
■ Monetary transmission operates primarily through risk appetite shocks, which generate large and persistent contractionary effects on output and employment.
■ Risk-free rate shocks alone have small, imprecisely estimated macro effects, indicating that standard policy surprises miss key transmission channels.

Why it matters

This study informs US macro and cross-asset strategy by re-evaluating how Federal Reserve announcements impact financial markets and the macroeconomy. Strategists evaluating FOMC meetings should look beyond changes in short-term interest rate expectations and track risk-sensitive asset prices directly. The findings suggest that communication driving market risk premia may have a larger impact on economic growth and inflation than shifts in the expected policy rate path, complicating simple rate-based modeling of monetary policy transmission.

Classified developed markets. The paper focuses on US monetary policy transmission by analyzing financial and macroeconomic data around Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings.

TransversalAmerican Economic Review, 1 Nov 2026

The Inflation Attention Threshold and Inflation Surges

Oliver Pfäuti

This paper examines how public attention to inflation shifts when inflation rises. The author estimates an attention threshold at a 4 percent inflation rate, above which attention doubles. During high-attention regimes, supply shocks exert stronger and more persistent upward pressure on inflation. Pfäuti develops a macroeconomic model incorporating this threshold to explain macroeconomic dynamics. The framework demonstrates how state-dependent attention accounts for rapid inflation surges, tight co-movement between actual inflation and inflation expectations, and a prolonged last mile of disinflation back toward central bank targets.

Key takeaways

■ Attention to inflation doubles when the inflation rate exceeds an estimated threshold of 4 percent.
■ Supply shocks exert stronger and more persistent effects on inflation during periods of high public attention.
■ Threshold-driven attention changes jointly explain inflation surges, co-movement with expectations, and a protracted disinflation path.

Why it matters

This research informs monetary policy analysis and inflation forecasting across fixed income and foreign exchange markets. Macro strategists tracking central bank reaction functions can use the 4 percent threshold concept to evaluate non-linear inflation dynamics. Furthermore, the finding that high-attention regimes amplify the persistence of supply shocks offers valuable context for modeling disinflation trajectories and pricing inflation-linked securities during structural policy shifts.

Classified transversal. The abstract presents a general macroeconomic model and empirical findings without citing specific countries or regional dataset settings.

Developed marketsBoard of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 30 Oct 2026

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

Hoopes, Charlotte; Zer, Ilknur; Uysal, Pinar

This paper examines whether sovereign risk continues to affect European banks a decade after major regulatory overhauls aimed at breaking the sovereign-bank nexus. Using daily credit default swap spreads and supervisory data on domestic sovereign portfolios, the authors find that the link persists but depends heavily on specific conditions. Banks with larger mark-to-market sovereign exposures, weaker capital positions, wholesale funding models, and operations in peripheral countries react more sharply to home-sovereign risk, particularly during periods of market stress and low fiscal capacity.

Key takeaways

■ Banks with larger domestic sovereign holdings exhibit higher sensitivity to home-sovereign risk, especially via mark-to-market exposures.
■ Transmission is amplified for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks during periods of elevated market uncertainty.
■ Post-crisis European policy reforms have reduced, but not eliminated, the operational link between sovereigns and banks.

Why it matters

This study informs sovereign and bank credit analysis across Europe, providing empirical backing for how balance-sheet structures and accounting classifications transmit sovereign stress to financial institutions. Rates and credit strategists tracking peripheral European debt or bank bond spreads can use these findings to better gauge contagion risks during periods of fiscal pressure or heightened market volatility.

Classified developed markets. The abstract explicitly studies post-crisis Europe, focusing on European sovereign and bank credit default swap spreads and supervisory data.

Read the paper   Topics: Sovereign risk, Banking, Financial regulation

 

Developed marketsDeutsche Bundesbank, 30 Oct 2026

What volatility reveals

Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

This paper examines the financial market transmission of the April 2025 U.S. tariff announcements, focusing on the unusual combination of a depreciating U.S. dollar and rising long-term Treasury yields. Using a structural model that exploits time-varying volatility and non-Gaussian excess kurtosis to identify shocks without traditional event restrictions, the authors evaluate whether a single tariff shock can explain market dynamics. The findings indicate that while an initial safe-haven reaction occurs, subsequent market behavior is driven by multiple distinct macroeconomic and financial forces.

Key takeaways

■ The initial market reaction to the tariff announcements aligns with a conventional safe-haven shock.
■ A single tariff shock fails to explain the subsequent joint behavior of exchange rates and long-term U.S. Treasury yields.
■ Financial segments are driven by distinct shocks, including safe-haven demand, U.S. institutional confidence, Treasury intermediation, and dollar convenience value.

Why it matters

This research informs rates, foreign exchange, and credit strategists analyzing the transmission of trade policy shocks and safe-haven asset dynamics. By decomposing the simultaneous movement of a weaker dollar and higher long-term yields into specific structural forces—such as Treasury-market intermediation and shifts in dollar convenience value—the study provides a framework for evaluating complex cross-asset reactions during heightened geopolitical and policy uncertainty. It directly benefits macro strategists modeling multi-factor market responses.

Classified developed markets. The paper focuses specifically on U.S. tariff announcements, U.S. Treasury yields, and the U.S. dollar.

Read the paper   Topics: Trade, Foreign exchange, Fixed income

 

TransversalEuropean Central Bank, 27 Oct 2026

The Tipping Point: Interest Rates and Financial Stability

Davide Porcellacchia

This ECB working paper examines how interest rate changes affect bank stability through a standard bank-run model. When interest rates rise, they reduce the market value of banks' long-term assets while simultaneously widening their net interest margins earned from providing liquidity-risk insurance to depositors. The paper identifies the specific condition determining which effect dominates—defined by the maturity mismatch adjusted for deposit duration—and models the exact interest rate level that triggers bank instability. Quantitative findings indicate that a negative effective maturity mismatch can create a lower bound for rates.

Key takeaways

■ Higher interest rates reduce long-term asset values but widen banks' interest margins.
■ Interest-rate risk is captured by banks' maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of deposits.
■ Quantification points to a potential negative effective maturity mismatch, making the instability tipping point a lower bound on interest rates.

Why it matters

This paper informs research on bank funding costs, interest rate risk management, and financial stability surveillance. Rates and credit strategists assessing bank vulnerabilities to rapid monetary tightening can use the duration-adjusted maturity mismatch framework to evaluate how higher policy rates simultaneously pressure asset portfolios and expand net interest margins. It is particularly relevant for monitoring tail risks in the banking sector and evaluating regulatory measures addressing deposit stickiness.

Classified transversal. The abstract outlines a theoretical framework regarding bank stability and interest rates without naming any specific countries, regions, or empirical data.

Developed marketsBanco de España, 27 Oct 2026

Measuring Euro-Area Inflation Risk with Options: Market-Implied Distribution and Balance of Risks

Diego Bonelli

This Banco de España working paper recovers risk-neutral spot and forward distributions of euro-area inflation from caps and floors. Comparing parametric SABR smile and nonparametric estimators against a normal benchmark, the author finds that non-normal models reveal significantly skewed and fat-tailed inflation risks. A balance-of-risks index derived from these distributions fluctuates rapidly at short horizons but moves slowly over longer ones. Interestingly, this market-implied balance of risks tends to sit lower than professional forecaster surveys on matched dates.

Key takeaways

■ Non-normal models (SABR and nonparametric) generate skewed, fat-tailed euro-area inflation distributions from options data.
■ The option-implied balance of risks varies sharply at short horizons and remains comparatively stable at long horizons.
■ Market-implied balance of risks is generally lower than professional forecaster measures on matched dates.

Why it matters

This paper informs euro-area inflation dynamics and rates markets, specifically pricing for inflation caps and floors. By extracting risk-neutral distributions that account for skewness and fat tails, fixed-income strategists and options traders gain a clearer view of tail risks compared to standard normal benchmarks. The findings also provide a quantitative benchmark to contrast market-implied inflation expectations with professional survey forecasts.

Classified developed markets. The paper explicitly studies euro-area inflation using caps, floors, and professional forecasts tied to the region.

Emerging marketsBank for International Settlements (BIS), 16 Oct 2026

Offshore forwards and exchange rate transmission in emerging markets

Gerardo Ferrara, Andreas Schrimpf, Ganesh Viswanath-Natraj, Zijie Wang

This study examines the transmission of exchange rate pressures from offshore non-deliverable forward markets to domestic currency markets in emerging economies. Using transaction-level data covering centrally cleared non-deliverable forwards across ten emerging-market currencies from 2013 to 2025, the authors analyze how offshore order flows impact pricing dynamics. The findings show that order flow in offshore markets directly influences both offshore forward and domestic spot exchange rates, while also widening the spread between offshore and onshore forward rates.

Key takeaways

■ Offshore non-deliverable forward order flow moves both offshore forward and domestic spot exchange rates.
■ Trading activity in offshore markets actively widens the offshore-onshore forward spread for emerging market currencies.
■ The study utilizes transaction-level data on centrally cleared non-deliverable forwards across ten emerging-market currencies from 2013 to 2025.

Why it matters

This paper is relevant for emerging market foreign exchange strategists and researchers tracking currency transmission mechanisms between onshore and offshore venues. By quantifying how offshore non-deliverable forward order flows impact domestic spot rates and basis spreads, the findings assist in monitoring currency pressure points, hedging dynamics, and market segmentation across emerging market jurisdictions.

Classified emerging markets. The paper explicitly studies ten emerging-market currencies using transaction-level data on non-deliverable forwards.
