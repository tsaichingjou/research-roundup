Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices

King, Thomas B.; Feldman, Corey

This paper investigates how uncertainty about the future conduct of monetary policy affects financial asset prices. Using U.S. data from 1994 to 2026, the authors compare asset volatility on days with Federal Open Market Committee communications to blackout periods. They find that policy communication acts as an independent risk factor. A New Keynesian structural model shows that monetary-policy risk raises risk premia on equities and nominal bonds, accounts for a large share of inflation-indexed bond risk premia, and depresses asset prices during periods of elevated uncertainty.

Key takeaways

■ Monetary-policy risk contributes 1.7 percentage points to the quarterly standard deviation of stock prices and 12 basis points to the 10-year nominal yield over the 1994–2026 sample.
■ A New Keynesian model indicates that monetary-policy risk adds about 40 basis points to the average 10-year nominal term premium and 120 basis points to the equity premium.
■ During the rapid tightening cycle starting in 2022, policy risk added roughly 35 basis points to the 10-year real term premium and lowered equity prices by about 4 percent.

Why it matters

This research provides a quantitative framework for rates, FX, and equity strategists seeking to isolate policy uncertainty from broader macroeconomic shocks. It helps explain the behavior of real and nominal term premia, inflation-risk premia, and equity volatility around central bank communications. The findings are particularly relevant for assessing how effective lower bound constraints and sudden shifts in reaction functions transmit financial tightening across asset classes.

Classified developed markets. The study relies on U.S. Federal Reserve data, primary dealer surveys, and Federal Open Market Committee communication schedules.

Developed marketsFederal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, 18 Sep 2026

Risk Appetite and Monetary Transmission

Schrimpf, Andreas; Schmeling, Maik; Bauer, Michael D.

Federal Reserve policy actions affect the economy primarily by shifting investor risk appetite rather than through interest rates alone. Analyzing 245 Federal Open Market Committee announcements between January 1996 and September 2025, the authors construct a high-frequency measure of market risk appetite across equities, credit, volatility, and foreign exchange. Rate surprises explain only 19% of changes in risk appetite. In structural models separating rate shocks from risk appetite shocks, shifts in risk appetite drive substantial macroeconomic changes—lowering output and inflation while raising unemployment—whereas pure interest-rate shocks have small and inconsistent effects. Monetary transmission thus operates predominantly via financial asset pricing and risk premia.

Key takeaways

■ Conventional interest-rate surprises explain only 19% of the variation in risk appetite shifts across 245 FOMC announcements from January 1996 to September 2025.
■ In a joint proxy SVAR, policy-induced risk appetite shocks drive large, persistent drops in output and inflation alongside higher unemployment, while risk-free rate shocks have negligible macroeconomic effects.
■ Forecast-error variance decompositions show risk appetite shocks account for 10–40% of the variance in US output, prices, and unemployment, compared to at most 5% for rate-path shocks.

Why it matters

This research is directly relevant for US rates, credit, and equity strategists evaluating central bank transmission. It demonstrates that tracking short-rate yield curve changes alone misses the primary channel of FOMC communications. Strategists can use these findings to focus on broader risk-asset reactions and risk premia—including credit spreads and equity volatility—when forecasting the macroeconomic impact of Fed policy decisions, rather than relying strictly on interest-rate path surprises.

Classified developed markets. The empirical analysis relies exclusively on US Federal Reserve announcements, US asset market indicators, and US aggregate economic data from 1996 to 2025.

Developed marketsAmerican Economic Review, 1 Nov 2026

The Inflation Attention Threshold and Inflation Surges

Oliver Pfäuti

This paper investigates how public attention to inflation changes and how those shifts drive macroeconomic outcomes. Using U.S. data from 1978 to 2024, the author estimates an inflation attention threshold at an annualized rate of 4 percent. When inflation crosses this level, public attention doubles. Consequently, adverse supply shocks generate stronger and more persistent inflationary pressures during high-attention periods. The author develops a theoretical model showing that attention dynamics help explain inflation surges, the shifting relationship with inflation expectations, and the prolonged 'last mile' of disinflation.

Key takeaways

■ The author estimates an inflation attention threshold at an annualized inflation rate of 4 percent, finding that attention roughly doubles when inflation exceeds this level.
■ Using U.S. data from 1978 to 2024, the study shows supply shocks have stronger and more persistent effects on inflation during times of high attention.
■ Shifting attention dynamics provide a joint explanation for inflation surges, co-movement with inflation expectations, and a prolonged last mile of disinflation.

Why it matters

This paper is relevant for inflation, rates, and FX strategists analyzing the transmission of supply shocks and the persistence of price pressures in developed economies. The findings suggest that once inflation crosses a 4 percent threshold, expectations become more sensitive to inflation data, amplifying the impact of supply shocks and delaying disinflation. This helps explain why central banks may face a stubborn 'last mile' in returning inflation to target, informing policy reaction function assessments and yield curve pricing.

Classified developed markets. The study's data, empirical estimation, and institutional setting focus explicitly on the United States and advanced economies.

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

Hoopes, Charlotte; Zer, Ilknur; Uysal, Pinar

This Federal Reserve Board paper investigates whether sovereign risk continues to affect European banks a decade after major institutional reforms were introduced. Using daily bank and sovereign credit default swap spreads alongside supervisory portfolio data, the authors examine how sovereign shocks transmit to bank risk and equity valuations. They find that the sovereign-bank relationship remains economically meaningful, particularly for institutions holding large domestic sovereign portfolios. Transmission is especially strong for mark-to-market exposures, less-capitalized banks, wholesale-funded institutions, peripheral country banks, and sovereigns with limited fiscal space.

Key takeaways

■ The sample covers 34 European banks across 12 countries from October 2014 to December 2025 using daily credit default swap spreads and semiannual supervisory data.
■ Transmission of sovereign risk to bank risk is substantially stronger for mark-to-market sovereign exposures than for amortized-cost holdings, reflecting immediate recognition of valuation losses.
■ Sovereign-to-bank transmission is stronger for less-capitalized, wholesale-funded, and peripheral banks, during elevated market uncertainty, and when home sovereigns have less fiscal capacity.

Why it matters

This study directly informs rates, credit, and sovereign debt strategists monitoring European banking sector vulnerabilities and peripheral sovereign spreads. The findings demonstrate that measuring sovereign risk exposure requires looking beyond aggregate debt holdings to examine accounting treatment (mark-to-market versus amortized cost), bank capital buffers, and sovereign fiscal space. The research is useful for assessing bank credit default swaps and equity valuations during periods of heightened market volatility or fiscal stress.

Classified developed markets. The paper uses data exclusively from European advanced economies and analyzes the post-crisis institutional architecture of the euro area.

What volatility reveals

Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

This paper investigates why the April 2025 U.S. tariff announcements produced an unusual combination of dollar depreciation and rising long-term U.S. Treasury yields, defying traditional safe-haven dynamics. Using a structural vector autoregression model estimated on daily data from January 2008 to May 2025, the authors examine whether the market reaction stems from a single trade shock or multiple distinct disturbances. They exploit time-varying volatility and excess kurtosis to recover macro-financial shocks without imposing exclusion restrictions.

Key takeaways

■ Initial financial market reactions to the April 2025 tariff announcements aligned with a conventional safe-haven shock, but this mechanism cannot explain subsequent exchange rate and Treasury yield behavior.
■ The joint dollar depreciation and rise in long-term Treasury yields were primarily driven by a repricing of U.S. dollar assets linked to U.S. institutional credibility.
■ A distinct Treasury-market intermediation shock accounted for the deterioration in swap spreads and Treasury convenience values during the episode.

Why it matters

This research informs rates, FX, and macro-credit strategists analyzing the transmission of trade policy shocks and the international role of dollar-denominated safe assets. By decomposing asset-price movements into orthogonal macro-financial forces—such as institutional credibility, safe-haven demand, and Treasury market intermediation—the findings help portfolio managers and economists disentangle competing structural drivers behind unusual co-movements in exchange rates and long-term yields.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses specifically on the United States and the euro area, examining U.S. tariff announcements, U.S. Treasuries, the U.S. dollar, and G10 sovereign bond yields.

TransversalEuropean Central Bank, 27 Oct 2026

The Tipping Point: Interest Rates and Financial Stability

Davide Porcellacchia

This paper models the net impact of interest rate changes on bank stability. Rates exert two opposing forces: higher interest rates reduce the value of long-term assets on bank balance sheets, but they also expand net interest margins by allowing banks to price liquidity risk. The dominating effect depends on the bank's maturity mismatch adjusted for deposit duration. Notably, theoretical quantification shows this effective mismatch can be negative. In such cases, the threshold for bank instability becomes a lower bound on interest rates, meaning excessively low rates threaten financial stability.

Key takeaways

■ Interest rates exert two opposing effects on banks: depressing long-term asset values while widening net interest margins from liquidity insurance.
■ A bank's overall exposure to interest-rate risk is governed by its maturity mismatch adjusted for the effective duration of deposits.
■ Quantification shows effective maturity mismatch can be negative, causing the stability tipping point to act as a lower bound on interest rates.

Why it matters

The framework provides central bank and financial sector strategists with a theoretical lens to evaluate financial stability across interest rate cycles. By highlighting how deposit duration counterbalances asset duration, the paper informs policy debates on the risks of low-rate environments versus rapid tightening cycles. Rates and bank credit analysts can use the concept of effective maturity mismatch to evaluate how deposit pricing dynamics affect bank solvency thresholds under varying macro regimes.

Classified transversal. The paper develops a theoretical bank-run model without using data from or focusing on any specific country or region.

Measuring Euro-Area Inflation Risk with Options: Market-Implied Distribution and Balance of Risks

Diego Bonelli

This paper estimates the market-implied probabilities of euro-area inflation outcomes using options data from caps and floors. The author compares non-normal models—the SABR smile and a nonparametric estimator—against a standard normal model, constructing forward distributions via copulas. The non-normal frameworks reveal that euro-area inflation distributions are skewed and fat-tailed, meaning standard normal models understate the probability of extreme inflation outcomes. Summarising these distributions via a balance-of-risks index, the paper finds that market-implied risks fluctuate sharply over short horizons but stay more stable over longer horizons, while consistently remaining below survey-based risk measures from professional forecasters.

Key takeaways

■ Non-normal models (SABR and nonparametric) show that euro-area option-implied inflation distributions are skewed and fat-tailed compared to normal benchmark models.
■ The constructed balance-of-risks index displays high short-horizon volatility but shows more limited movement over longer horizons.
■ Option-implied measures of the balance of inflation risks are generally lower than corresponding risk estimates from professional forecasters.

Why it matters

This study is directly relevant to euro-area inflation and rates strategists monitoring market-implied tail risks. By demonstrating that normal models underestimate extreme inflation probabilities, the framework helps refine the interpretation of inflation caps, floors, and copula-derived forward distributions. Furthermore, the systematic gap between market-implied risk measures and professional forecaster surveys provides fixed income strategists with useful context when comparing market pricing against consensus macro projections across different horizons.

Classified developed markets. The study focuses specifically on euro-area inflation risk and uses market data from euro-area inflation options (caps and floors).

Emerging marketsBank for International Settlements (BIS), 16 Oct 2026

Offshore forwards and exchange rate transmission in emerging markets

Gerardo Ferrara, Andreas Schrimpf, Ganesh Viswanath-Natraj, Zijie Wang

This study examines how offshore non-deliverable forward (NDF) markets transmit price pressures to domestic currency markets in emerging economies. Using transaction-level data on centrally cleared NDFs for ten emerging-market currencies from 2013 to 2025, the authors show that offshore order flow significantly impacts both offshore forward and domestic spot exchange rates. When offshore investors buy US dollars against an emerging-market currency, dealers hedge these positions by purchasing dollars in the domestic spot market. Capital controls restrict cross-border arbitrage, creating a persistent spread between offshore and onshore forward rates that widens when dealer balance sheets are constrained.

Key takeaways

■ A $1 billion long-USD offshore NDF order flow depreciates the domestic spot exchange rate by approximately 10 basis points and the offshore NDF rate by about 18 basis points.
■ Domestic spot price impact is state-dependent, falling from 17.8 basis points when dealer FX positions are 1 SD below mean to 7.1 basis points when 1 SD above mean.
■ Regulatory changes altering domestic banks' access to offshore NDF markets, as seen in India (2020, 2026) and Malaysia (2016), directly drive cross-market arbitrage and forward spread widening.

Why it matters

These findings are relevant for FX strategists and macro investors monitoring emerging-market currency dynamics and policy restrictions. Understanding how NDF order flow spills over into onshore spot markets helps explain intraday volatility and price discovery in currencies like INR, KRW, and CNY. Furthermore, the results demonstrate how regulatory decisions regarding domestic bank participation in offshore markets directly influence market segmentation, cross-border arbitrage capacity, and exchange rate pass-through.

Classified emerging markets. The paper specifically investigates exchange rate transmission and market segmentation across ten emerging-market currencies using transaction-level data from 2013 to 2025.

How this issue was made

An automated agent screened 732 new publications from 54 sources (3 Oct 2026 to 3 Nov 2026). 684 with a usable abstract were scored for market relevance by a language model, and the 8 above were selected, with at most two papers per source.

For 48 papers whose feed carried no abstract, a stand-in was recovered before screening: from OpenAlex, the publisher's page, or the opening of the paper's own text.

37 publications with no abstract anywhere were labelled by market focus from their titles only (17 from Journal of Political Economy, 7 from Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 7 from Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, 4 from Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, and others). A title is too little to rank on, so they were not eligible for this issue.

Left out as not research papers: 1 from Banque de France (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Banco de Portugal (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Bank of Canada (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Bank of Japan (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Deutsche Bundesbank (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Central Bank of Ireland (dataset, not a paper); 1 from European Central Bank (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Journal of Political Economy (not a research paper); 1 from Narodowy Bank Polski (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Sveriges Riksbank (dataset, not a paper); 1 from Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye (dataset, not a paper).

Market focus: Emerging markets for research centred on emerging or developing economies, Developed markets for advanced economies, and Transversal for work spanning both or tied to neither.

Summaries are written by a language model from the full text of 6 of the 8 papers (openly published PDF or web page) and from the abstract for the rest, and may contain errors. Check the original paper before relying on a finding.
